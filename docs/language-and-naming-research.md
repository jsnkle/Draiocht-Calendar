# Language and naming research

These notes record the evidence behind the [month names](month-names.md), checked on 2026-09-19. I want the names to describe the solar year clearly, with Ré Anann serving as a dedication. A word can be valid Irish and still be a poor fit for its place in the sequence.

The main language sources are Ó Dónaill's *Foclóir Gaeilge–Béarla* (1977), the New English–Irish Dictionary, and *An Caighdeán Oifigiúil* (2017). The historical material uses eDIL, manuscript transcriptions of *Sanas Cormaic*, and a scholarly study of the Paps of Anu. Links below identify the entries and passages supporting each conclusion.

## The single-word names

The meanings of **Éirí**, **Neartú**, **Iompú**, **Ísliú**, **Doimhniú**, and **Filleadh** have direct dictionary support. There is no linguistic reason in these entries to discard them as standalone names.

| Name | Evidence | Assessment of its role |
|---|---|---|
| Éirí | [Entry, senses 1–2](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fgb/%C3%A9ir%C3%AD): a verbal noun and a noun for rising, with solar examples. | A reasonable image for the rising daily solar path after the vernal equinox. It isn't a technical term for increasing solar declination. |
| Neartú | [Entry](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fgb/neart%C3%BA): the verbal noun of *neartaigh*, also strengthening or reinforcement. | Fits the increasing presence of daylight. “Strengthening” is an interpretation of the season, not a measurement of the Sun's intrinsic power. |
| Iompú | [Entry, senses 1–2](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fgb/iomp%C3%BA): turning, including changes of direction. | Fits the phase following the solstice. The actual turn occurs in the preceding solstice month. |
| Ísliú | [Entry](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fgb/%C3%ADsli%C3%BA): lowering or descent. | Fits the lower daily solar path later in the year. |
| Doimhniú | [*Doimhnigh*](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fgb/doimhnigh) explicitly gives its verbal noun in *-iú* and examples of deepening. | The form and meaning are supported even though the dictionary doesn't give it a separate full headword. Deepening darkness is the intended image. |
| Filleadh | [Entry, sense 3(a)](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fgb/filleadh): return; other senses include folding. | The return toward longer daylight is a valid selected sense. A word having other meanings does not invalidate this use. |

These images follow a Northern Hemisphere, temperate-season perspective. A lunar boundary doesn't begin a new stage of the Sun's motion; the name describes the broader phase in which the lunation falls.

## Idir-Ré

The dictionary attests [*idir-ré*](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fgb/idir-r%C3%A9) as “interlunar period.” It is an existing word, including the hyphen, rather than a phrase assembled without precedent. The [*ré* entry](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fgb/r%C3%A9) includes both lunar and temporal senses.

Neither entry defines a month spanning an equinox or the change of solar year. That is the meaning I give Idir-Ré in this calendar. “Between-time” expresses my interpretation of the name.

## Grian Thuaidh and Grian Theas

[*Grian*](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fgb/grian) means Sun. Ó Dónaill labels [*thuaidh*](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fgb/thuaidh) and [*theas*](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fgb/theas) as both adverbs and adjectives. The newer dictionary independently supplies these adjectives under [north](https://www.focloir.ie/en/dictionary/ei/north) and [south](https://www.focloir.ie/en/dictionary/ei/south), with examples following nouns. The spelling *thuaidh* is the adjective's listed form; it is not an accidental mutation of an invented adjective.

My reading of the two names as **Northern Sun** and **Southern Sun** applies that attested noun-and-adjective pattern. I use them as newly chosen calendar names, with their astronomical reference explained below.

The more significant question is what they communicate. I intend the Sun's northern and southern extremes relative to the celestial equator, not a claim that an observer must look north at the summer solstice. This distinction follows the astronomical description of the [seasons and the Earth's orbit](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/faq/seasons_orbit).

The ordinary Irish term for a solstice is [*grianstad*](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fgb/grianstad); the New English–Irish Dictionary gives [*grianstad an gheimhridh*](https://www.focloir.ie/en/dictionary/ei/solstice) for the winter solstice. Grian Thuaidh and Grian Theas are poetic names with a supported construction, rather than translations of the word “solstice.” I see a basis for retaining them with the intended reference explained.

## Cothromú, Réiteach, and the approach to the equinox

[*Cothromú*](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fgb/cothrom%C3%BA) is the verbal noun of *cothromaigh* and carries the meaning of equalization or balance. Its sense as a process helps explain the original choice for a month approaching the equinox. That explanation is linguistically possible, but the earlier placement created an expectation that this was the equinox month itself.

[*Réiteach*](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fgb/r%C3%A9iteach) has senses of clearing, preparation, and settling or resolving something. The dictionary supports reconciliation in context. It doesn't make Réiteach a word for an equinox, or establish balance as its defining seasonal meaning.

I adopted the following revision:

| Position | Previous name | Current name | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Second ordinary month after the summer-solstice month | Cothromú | **Giorrú** | Describes shortening daylight without suggesting the equinox has arrived. |
| Month containing the autumnal equinox | Réiteach | **Cothromú** | Places the image of balancing at the crossing it most readily suggests. |

[*Giorrú*](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fgb/giorr%C3%BA) is the verbal noun of [*giorraigh*](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fgb/giorraigh), to shorten. The newer dictionary's [*shorten* entry](https://www.focloir.ie/en/dictionary/ei/shorten) distinguishes making something shorter from its becoming shorter, and includes shortening days. Giorrú is a newly chosen month name using an attested word.

[*Cónocht*](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fgb/c%C3%B3nocht) means equinox, and the entry explicitly supplies *cónocht an fhómhair* for the autumnal equinox. I considered that literal label and chose Cothromú for the image of balancing.

“Balance” remains an image. An equinox is not generally the local date of exactly equal daylight and darkness. [USNO explains the difference](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/faq/equinoxes).

## Why Síneadh replaces Aisling

[*Aisling*](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fgb/aisling) means a vision or dream, and also names a kind of vision poem. Its use for anticipation was understandable. It was the least directly solar of the ordinary names: it described a human response to the approach of spring.

I chose **Síneadh** to keep the vocabulary close to observable change. Its [dictionary entry](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fgb/s%C3%ADneadh) gives extension or prolongation and explicitly includes lengthening of the day. The newer [*lengthen* entry](https://www.focloir.ie/en/dictionary/ei/lengthen) confirms that use. This gives a seasonal basis more specific than an isolated translation of “stretching.”

The change from Aisling to Síneadh shifts the emphasis from anticipation to lengthening daylight and gives Giorrú a counterpart. Aisling's translation was sound; I changed it because I wanted a clearer solar description.

## Ré Anann

**Ré** has lunar and time-period meanings. **Anann** is a historical form associated with Ana/Anu, documented in names and phrases collected under [*Ana* in eDIL](https://dil.ie/3247), including the name of the Paps. My reading of **Ré Anann** as **Time of Anu** or **Moon of Anu** draws on those uses.

A noun followed by a person's name in the genitive is an established construction. *An Caighdeán Oifigiúil* §3.1.2(f) illustrates it with *oifig Áine*. Applying that construction here supports my dedication; it doesn't make the complete phrase an attested ancient month name. [Official grammar, printed p. 38](https://data.oireachtas.ie/ie/oireachtas/caighdeanOifigiul/2017/2017-08-03_an-caighdean-oifigiuil-2017_ga.pdf#page=65).

For the mythological reference, the [Early Irish Glossaries Database](https://www.asnc.cam.ac.uk/irishglossaries/concordances.php?cpFamily=sc&display=fulltext&main=18&page=4&perPage=10&readingID=) lets the Ana entry be compared across manuscript versions of *Sanas Cormaic*, including B.33, M.29, and Y.31. The text describes her as mother of the Irish gods, nourishing them, and associated with the two hills. This is direct textual evidence for the dedication's subject.

That evidence has limits. It is a medieval account, and its explanatory wordplay is not automatically a reliable account of a name's origin. Frédéric Armao's [2017 study, pp. 17–18](https://www.estudiosirlandeses.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/Art%C3%ADculo-1.pdf) discusses passages that relate Ana, Anann, Danann, and the Morrígan in different ways. It also reports competing etymological interpretations. That is insufficient grounds to collapse all the names into one unqualified identity or turn a proposed river-name connection into proof of worship across Europe.

The supported conclusion is narrower and useful: **Ré Anann can remain a modern dedication to an attested figure.** Its timing by the seasonal Black Moon is my design. The sources examined here do not connect Ana/Anu to that lunar rule.

## The two parts of Idir-Ré

[*Críoch*](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fgb/cr%C3%ADoch) includes ending or completion; [*tús*](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fgb/t%C3%BAs) means beginning. They are suitable words for those two ideas.

The older strings **Idir-Ré Críoch** and **Idir-Ré Tús** shouldn't be glossed as though the second nouns were Irish adjectives meaning “ending” and “beginning.” The entries give distinct genitive forms, *críche* and *túis*; a full phrase would require its intended grammatical relationship to be specified.

There is a straightforward way to preserve the labels without inventing that phrase: **Idir-Ré — Críoch** and **Idir-Ré — Tús**, with the second word explicitly serving as a separate section label. The ordinary instructions use “before equinox” and “after equinox.” None of these labels divides the lunation into two months.

## Pronunciation and use in dates

For pronunciation, I link to [Teanglann's recordings](https://www.teanglann.ie/en/fuaim/) where available instead of treating the old English phonetic spellings as authoritative. The dictionary offers different dialect recordings; an English approximation can conceal those differences.

This review concerns names used as standalone labels in an English-language calendar. It does not supply complete Irish sentences for numbered dates, where grammatical context matters. No fluent-speaker consultation has taken place. A future review can focus on how these specific coined names sound and work in use, rather than rechecking whether every ordinary word exists.
