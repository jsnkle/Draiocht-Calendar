# History and sources

I want to be clear about what this calendar is built on. Some parts follow astronomical events. Some draw on Irish language and traditions. Others are choices I've made because they help me relate to the year.

I'm not an expert in these fields. This is an idea I'm developing in public, and I welcome corrections supported by sources. I want to be able to explain why a claim belongs here, and to change it when the evidence calls for that.

## What I've brought to the calendar

The equinox-to-equinox year, months beginning with the sunset-to-sunset day containing astronomical new moon, Idir-Ré, and the use of the seasonal Black Moon are the structure I chose. The [Natural Tuning](natural-tuning.md) sequence comes from my reflective practice. I also use angular solar positions for the four cross-quarter markers.

My contribution is the way I've brought these ideas together. I haven't established this combination as an ancient calendar. Its value to me comes from using it, observing the cycles it follows, and seeing whether it helps me stay connected to them.

## What the historical sources support

**Druids and astronomy.** Caesar describes Druids discussing celestial bodies and their movements in *Gallic War* 6.14. I begin each day at sunset, following the night-before-day reckoning Caesar describes among the Gauls in 6.18. That account doesn't establish my new-moon-day rule, a New Year at the vernal equinox, or Idir-Ré. I also have to read Caesar as a Roman author writing about peoples he fought. [Primary text, chapters 14 and 18](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Caesar/Gallic_War/6B%2A.html).

**A different lunar reference.** Pliny's *Natural History* 16.250 connects the sixth day of the Moon with the beginnings of months and years in his account of the Druids. That is a reason for me to distinguish my new-moon rule from a recovered Druidic practice. [Primary text, section 250](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Pliny_the_Elder/16%2A.html).

**The Coligny calendar.** This surviving Gaulish calendar records a lunisolar system spanning five years, with months of 29 or 30 days and intercalary months. It is valuable material evidence. I can't dismiss its mathematics as inauthentic merely because the surviving object comes from the Roman period, or use it to claim an earlier system without calculation. [Lugdunum museum](https://lugdunum.grandlyon.com/Oeuvre-incontournable/2381-Calendrier-gaulois-de-Coligny).

**Seasonal festivals.** The National Museum of Ireland describes Brigid's Day on February 1, Bealtaine on May 1, Lúnasa on August 1, and Samhain on November 1. Its folklife material includes rural traditions of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Those observances have their own history and community meaning. My preference for astronomical markers doesn't make their dates mistakes. [Museum account](https://cms.museum.ie/en-IE/News/2023/St-Brigids-Day).

**Ancient monuments.** Newgrange dates to around 3200 BCE. Its solar alignment shows an interest in the Sun, but doesn't establish later Druidic calendar rules. Heritage Ireland also describes illumination at the Mound of the Hostages around Samhain and Imbolc. My earlier claim that no such alignments were known was too broad. Neither example settles when a community began its year. [Newgrange chronology](https://heritageireland.ie/articles/mesolithic-iron-age-8000bc-400ad/); [Tara alignment](https://heritageireland.ie/2023/09/samhain-the-roots-of-halloween/).

## Why I begin the year at the vernal equinox

For me, Samhain begins reflection. Imbolc brings a sense of action, and the vernal equinox brings a sense of growth. The vernal equinox in the Northern Hemisphere also gives me a precise solar event from which to reckon the year. Those are my reasons for choosing it.

The historical question is more complicated. In *The Wooing of Emer*, paragraph 55, the year is described in two parts: summer from Beltaine and winter from Samuin. That passage supports a seasonal division; it doesn't explicitly say that either festival begins the calendar year. [Kuno Meyer's translation, hosted by University College Cork](https://celt.ucc.ie/published/T301021/text056.html).

Ronald Hutton's 2024 study, *The Celtic New Year and Feast of the Dead*, examines the sources behind the Samhain New Year claim. He concludes that the evidence is indirect and inconclusive, while allowing that the claim has not been conclusively disproved. That gives me grounds to question a statement of certainty, not to declare another date historically proven. [*Folklore* 135(1), 69–86](https://doi.org/10.1080/0015587X.2023.2282282).

I don't need to settle that debate to explain my own choice. I also don't want to turn my preference into a claim that I have corrected other people's traditions.

## Ré Anann and its dedication

There is a textual basis for the dedication to Ana/Anu. *Sanas Cormaic* describes Ana as the mother of the gods of Ireland and associates her with the two hills known as the Paps of Anu. The historical dictionary records the related Anann form. [Manuscript versions](https://www.asnc.cam.ac.uk/irishglossaries/concordances.php?cpFamily=sc&display=fulltext&main=18&page=4&perPage=10&readingID=); [eDIL, *Ana*](https://dil.ie/3247).

My earlier notes went further, treating Anu and Danu as interchangeable and the Danube as evidence of her worship across the Celtic world. I don't have sufficient grounds for those statements. The sources contain overlapping names and interpretations; they don't justify presenting one unified account as settled. [Frédéric Armao's study of the Paps and Cathair Crobh Dearg, especially pp. 17–18](https://www.estudiosirlandeses.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/03/Art%C3%ADculo-1.pdf).

I can retain Ré Anann as a modern dedication without making those larger claims. Its connection to the Black Moon is my choice. The [language research](language-and-naming-research.md#ré-anann) explains the wording.

## What the astronomy gives me

| Feature | Basis and practical limit |
|---|---|
| Months follow new moons. | [USNO's phase service](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/data/MoonPhases) supplies calculated instants. [NASA](https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/help/moonorbit.html) explains the roughly 29.53-day mean and its variation. |
| The year follows the vernal equinox. | The boundary is a solar event. It isn't generally the date of exactly equal daylight and darkness. [USNO explanation](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/faq/equinoxes). |
| Cross-quarter markers follow angular progress. | The chosen positions are 45°, 135°, 225°, and 315° along the ecliptic from the vernal equinox. Solar longitude already has a role in other calendars. [Hong Kong Observatory](https://www.hko.gov.hk/en/gts/time/24solarterms.htm). |
| There is no fixed leap schedule. | This follows from my definitions: the events set the boundaries. Partial months, variable counts, and naming conventions still need to be handled. |
| Event boundaries can follow changing cycles. | Lunar laser ranging measures changes in the Moon's orbit. Defining boundaries by events allows their durations to change, but predictions and tables still need updating. [NASA](https://www.nasa.gov/missions/laser-beams-reflected-between-earth-and-moon-boost-science/). |

For my purposes, following these events directly feels more grounded and useful than fitting them into a fixed arrangement of months. That is an advantage I experience, rather than proof that this calendar is best for every purpose. The Gregorian calendar also has an astronomical basis: it approximates the solar year. [USNO on the year and seasons](https://aa.usno.navy.mil/faq/seasons_orbit).

## What remains open

The [naming research](language-and-naming-research.md) led me to choose Giorrú for shortening daylight, move Cothromú to the autumnal-equinox month, and replace Aisling with Síneadh for lengthening daylight. The full grammar and pronunciation of dates spoken in Irish would need work beyond these names used as labels in English.

The daily rule is sunset to sunset, with day 1 containing the new moon. The reference tables use calculated sunsets for Orlando. I haven't defined a substitute day boundary for places and seasons without a daily sunset. The generated tables cover three complete solar years, and the naming convention hasn't been checked across a long span of actual astronomical data. Unusual arrangements retain each lunar month's boundaries even where a name is unsettled.

Sources were consulted on 2026-09-19. [Data provenance](../data/README.md) documents the numerical tables. If you can improve an interpretation, a translation, or a calculation, I welcome that contribution.
